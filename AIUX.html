Making the intent as visible as the interface

AI can generate a convincing interface in minutes. But a polished screen does not always explain why a feature exists, how a workflow is expected to behave, or which business rules sit behind it.

This case study explores a lightweight way to bridge that gap: a Design Intent Document (DID) that translates an AI-generated mockup into a structured explanation of its likely purpose, features, workflow, functional expectations, and assumptions.

Focus

AI-assisted UX workflows

My contribution

Research synthesis, workflow analysis, prompt design, and evaluation of the DID output

Working example

Pre-ETL Data Conditioning

Intervention

A concise, six-part Design Intent Document

The central question

Could a concise explanation of an AI-generated mockup help UX designers spend less effort interpreting the artifact and more effort validating whether the solution makes sense?

This was an exploratory exercise. The potential benefits are discussed as hypotheses and observations, not as measured time savings or proven business outcomes.

The challenge

AI makes it easier to get to a screen. Understanding the screen is a different task.

AI-generated requirements, prototypes, and mockups are increasingly becoming part of product and UX workflows. They can help teams visualize ideas early, explore possible solutions, and communicate concepts. But the generated interface may not carry the full context that shaped it.

A screen can look complete while leaving important questions unanswered:

  • What user or business need is this screen addressing?
  • Why does a particular control or feature exist?
  • What happens when a user changes a setting or encounters an error?
  • Which business rules are reflected in the interface, and which are missing?
  • Are the displayed interactions consistent with the existing product and domain?

What AI accelerates

  • Visualizing concepts
  • Generating interface examples
  • Exploring possible layouts and flows
  • Creating early prototypes and documentation

What still needs human judgment

  • Checking assumptions and business rules
  • Validating workflows and edge cases
  • Checking terminology and product consistency
  • Identifying missing context and gaps
The interface was visible. The intent wasn't always visible.

What the research surfaced

AI changes where UX effort is spent, not the need for UX judgment.

Team research explored how AI-generated requirements, prototypes, and mockups are entering UX workflows. Participants recognized the value of faster visualization and communication, but none considered AI outputs trustworthy without human review.

A shift in the workflow

Traditional flow

  1. Problem statement
  2. UX exploration
  3. Design solution

Emerging AI-assisted flow

  1. AI-generated prototype
  2. UX interprets intent
  3. UX validates assumptions
  4. UX identifies gaps
  5. UX redesigns or refines the solution

Five recurring observations

01. Faster visualization

AI can turn abstract requirements into something teams can see and discuss earlier.

02. Illusion of completeness

A polished mockup can appear finished even when requirements, workflows, and edge cases remain incomplete.

03. More interpretation work

UX has to reconstruct the intent behind the generated interface before it can confidently evaluate it.

04. Missing product context

AI may not have the full product history, domain constraints, existing workflows, or business rules.

05. Unclear ownership

Teams may treat the generated artifact as the requirement, leaving UX to determine what is accurate and what is assumed.

Designers are becoming an unofficial validation layer for AI-generated artifacts.

Putting the idea to work

A real example: Pre-ETL Data Conditioning

To explore the problem in a concrete workflow, I used a detailed Pre-ETL Data Conditioning Functional Requirements Document (FRD) as the source for an AI-generated HTML mockup, then examined what a DID could make easier to understand.

Starting point: a detailed FRD

The FRD covered several layers of product behavior, including:

  • File structure configuration
  • Transformation and mapping rules
  • Filtering rules and execution order
  • Validation logic and edge cases
  • Audit requirements and configuration management
  • Processing workflows and related permissions

From requirements to an interface

01. FRD

Detailed business and functional requirements describing what the system needs to support.

02. AI mockup

An HTML representation of the proposed experience, including configuration, rules, monitoring, and audit areas.

03. DID

A structured interpretation of what the generated interface appears to intend.

What the mockup represented

  • ETL Job configuration
  • Rule Management and Add/Edit Rule forms
  • File Structure Settings
  • Monitoring and Audit History
  • Pre-conditioned file download

Questions the interface alone did not answer clearly

  • Why is there a master toggle?
  • Why can rules be reordered?
  • Why can a rule refer to column names as well as column indexes?
  • Why is an audit screen needed?
  • Why does the workflow include downloading a pre-conditioned file?
  • How are the different configuration steps expected to work together?

The intervention: a Design Intent Document

Make the generated interface easier to understand, inspect, and question.

I explored a simple intervention: ask AI to analyze the generated mockup and produce a concise document that explains its likely intent. The DID is not another requirements document. It is an interpretation layer between the generated interface and the UX designer reviewing it.

The six parts of the DID

01. Screen Summary

What is the purpose of this screen, and what task is the user trying to complete?

02. Feature List

What key features and capabilities are shown or implied?

03. User Flow Description

What journey and major interactions does the interface appear to support?

04. Component Breakdown

What are the main UI elements, and what role does each appear to play?

05. Functional Requirements / User Stories

What functional expectations can be expressed from the generated interface?

06. Key Assumptions & Intended Behaviors

What defaults, validations, business rules, and expected behaviors appear to be assumed?

How I framed the prompt

Analyze the AI-generated mockup and create a concise Design Intent Document. Explain the likely intent of the design through a screen summary, feature list, user flow, component breakdown, functional requirements or user stories, and key assumptions and intended behaviors. Keep it brief and structured. Help Design, Product, and Engineering understand the intent without making unsupported assumptions.

Important: The word “likely” matters. The DID describes what can be inferred from the mockup. It does not turn an assumption into a confirmed business requirement.

What changes with the DID?

Without a DID

UX has to move between the FRD and mockup, infer purpose, reconstruct the workflow, and identify which behaviors need clarification.

With a DID

UX gets a structured starting point for understanding the generated interface, checking assumptions, and identifying what still needs validation.

The DID doesn't create new information. It makes relevant information easier to consume and validate.

Where the DID can help

Three practical use cases from the Pre-ETL example.

01. Understand the screen

Scenario: A UX designer receives the generated ETL Job and Rule Management screens.

DID contribution: The Screen Summary, Feature List, and User Flow provide a first explanation of what the screen is for and how it may be used.

Potential value: Faster orientation and less need to reverse-engineer the interface from visual elements alone.

02. Understand expected behavior

Scenario: The mockup shows configurable rules, but the interface alone does not explain all the conditions, validations, or expected behavior.

DID contribution: Functional Requirements and Key Assumptions make inferred expectations more explicit and easier to review.

Potential value: UX can move from guessing what a control might do to validating whether the inferred behavior matches the FRD and domain rules.

03. Align on meaning

Scenario: Product, UX, and Engineering may interpret the same generated screen differently.

DID contribution: A shared description of purpose, features, workflow, and assumptions gives the team a common starting point for discussion.

Potential value: More focused conversations about what is correct, missing, or still uncertain.

Benefits and trade-offs

Where it may help

  • Faster initial understanding of generated screens
  • Clearer visibility of inferred behavior and assumptions
  • A shared vocabulary across Design, Product, and Engineering
  • A more focused starting point for validation
  • Potentially less effort spent reconstructing intent

What it cannot solve

  • A DID can be wrong if the mockup is wrong.
  • It can become another layer of documentation.
  • It may reinforce solution anchoring.
  • It cannot confirm business rules or domain truth by itself.
  • It does not replace the FRD, UX discovery, or stakeholder review.

A useful guardrail

The DID should make the artifact easier to question, not harder to question.

Use it to surface assumptions and guide validation. Do not treat it as approval of the AI-generated solution or as a replacement for the source requirements.

Reflection and next steps

The value is not in documenting every screen. It is in making uncertainty easier to see.

When a DID is useful

  • When an AI-generated mockup is being handed to UX for review.
  • When the interface looks complete but its purpose or behavior is unclear.
  • When teams need a quick, shared interpretation before a deeper review.
  • When assumptions need to be separated from confirmed requirements.

When it is not enough

  • When the source requirements are missing or unreliable.
  • When domain rules need confirmation from subject matter experts.
  • When the team has not yet validated the underlying user problem.
  • When the generated interface itself needs to be challenged or rethought.

How I would evaluate it next

This exploration suggests a useful direction, but the impact needs to be tested. A next iteration could compare UX review with and without a DID using the same AI-generated artifact.

  • Time taken to understand the mockup and its workflow
  • Number and type of clarification questions raised
  • Assumptions and missing requirements identified
  • Accuracy of the DID against the source FRD and expert review
  • Whether the DID helps reviewers focus on meaningful UX decisions

What this means for UX

AI can generate an interface, but the generated interface still needs interpretation, validation, and judgment. The opportunity for UX is not simply to polish what AI creates. It is to understand the intent, question the assumptions, and make sure the solution addresses the right problem.

FRD provides depth. Mockup provides visualization. DID provides interpretation. UX provides judgment.

AI can generate the interface. UX still needs to understand the intent.

Case study note: This is an exploratory workflow study. No quantitative reduction in time, meetings, or rework has been established yet. Any future impact claims should be based on a comparative evaluation.
